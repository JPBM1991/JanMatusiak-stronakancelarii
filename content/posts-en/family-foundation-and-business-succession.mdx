A family foundation is sometimes presented as a solution that closes the subject of succession. Yet the Family Foundation Act makes clear that, as a rule, a foundation may not carry out operating business activity. It is therefore worth knowing the basic principles behind the structure of a family foundation before contributing assets to it.

## What does a family foundation actually do?

Under Article 2(1) of the Family Foundation Act, it is a legal person established for the purpose of accumulating assets, managing them in the interests of the beneficiaries, and providing benefits to them. The founder contributes assets to cover a founding fund with a value of no less than PLN 100,000 (Article 17), and the foundation may not return those assets to the founder, in whole or in part, unless the Act provides otherwise (Article 20).

From a succession perspective, the most important effect is what happens when assets are contributed to a family foundation. Assets contributed to the foundation cease to be the founder's property, so on the founder's death they do not form part of the estate, are not subject to division, and do not, as a rule, become the subject of a dispute between heirs. The foundation continues, because its existence is not tied to the founder's life. That is the genuine value of this institution.

The question is what cannot be contributed to the foundation.

## What may a family foundation not do?

Article 5(1) of the Act contains a closed list. A foundation may carry out business activity only in the following areas: disposing of assets, provided they were not acquired solely for further disposal; lease and tenancy; joining and participating in commercial companies, investment funds and cooperatives; trading in securities and derivatives; granting loans to companies in which it participates and to beneficiaries; dealing in currencies for the purposes of its own payments; non-industrial processing of plant and animal products; and forestry management. The last two it may carry out only in connection with an agricultural holding it operates (Article 5(2)).

What is absent from that list? Trade, industrial production, services, transport, construction, i.e. ordinary operating activity. A family foundation is an ownership and investment structure, not a business enterprise. Going beyond the list does not invalidate the foundation itself, but in the scope of such activity the income tax rate is 25% of the tax base ([Article 24r(1) of the CIT Act](/en/aktualnosci/family-foundation-presidential-veto-2026)), and the exemptions and deductions under Articles 17 to 18f of that Act do not apply to it (Article 24r(3)).

## So where does the business sit?

The business sits outside the foundation, in one of two arrangements.

The first is the target arrangement. The business operates in a company, and the foundation holds shares in it under Article 5(1)(3). The company carries out the activity, the foundation is its shareholder, and the shares do not form part of the estate because they no longer belong to the founder. This is the model that people have in mind when they speak of a foundation as a succession tool.

The second is the actual state of affairs for some founders. Certain components of personal assets have been contributed, but the business has not. The activity is still carried out as a sole proprietorship, or the shares have remained in personal assets. The foundation exists and covers the assets contributed to it. It does not touch the business in any way.

## What happens to what was left outside the foundation?

A sole proprietorship is inseparably tied to the person of the entrepreneur and ceases to exist on their death. Succession management allows the enterprise to be kept running, but by definition it is a temporary solution, a "bridge" for the duration of the probate proceedings, and not a target structure. [I have described it separately](/en/aktualnosci/succession-management-sole-proprietorship).

Shares in a limited liability company that have not been contributed to the foundation form part of the estate, but their fate is determined by the company's articles of association. Article 183 § 1 of the Commercial Companies Code allows the entry of heirs into the company in place of the deceased shareholder to be restricted or excluded. The articles must then specify the terms for buying out heirs who do not join the company, failing which the restriction itself is ineffective. Article 183 §§ 2 and 3 additionally allow the division of shares between heirs to be excluded or restricted.

In practice this leads to two extremes. Either the articles exclude the heirs, in which case they receive a cash payment instead of shares, which is sometimes the opposite of the founder's intention. Or the articles are silent, all the heirs join, and until the estate is divided they remain jointly entitled to the shareholding, exercising their rights in the company through a joint representative (Article 184 § 1 of the Commercial Companies Code). If they cannot agree on that person, the company may in effect be left without a functioning shareholder for that period.

## Who runs the foundation after the founder's death?

This is the second question that usually escapes attention. The foundation continues, but someone has to run it.

The founder's rights and obligations are non-transferable (Article 13(1) of the Act) and do not pass to the heirs. The founder may, however, entrust the exercise of their powers to another person in the statute, specifying the scope of that entrustment (Article 13(2)). The statute is therefore the only place where this can be arranged during the founder's lifetime.

If the statute does not regulate this, the statutory provisions apply. Management board members are appointed and dismissed by the founder, and after the founder's death by the supervisory board, provided one has been established. If the founder dies and there is no supervisory board, that power passes to the assembly of beneficiaries (Article 61(3)). Similarly, supervisory board members are appointed by the founder, and after the founder's death by the assembly of beneficiaries (Article 68(3)).

It should be noted that establishing a supervisory board is optional and only becomes mandatory once the number of beneficiaries exceeds twenty-five (Article 64). In a typical family foundation, with a few beneficiaries within the family, there simply is no supervisory board. Where the statute is silent, this means that after the founder's death the composition of the management board is decided by the assembly of beneficiaries, i.e. most often precisely those people between whom the foundation was meant to prevent a dispute.

## What about the legitim?

Contributing assets to a foundation does not automatically exclude them from legitim calculations. The founding fund is added to the estate when calculating the legitim (Article 993 § 2 of the Civil Code), and the foundation itself may be the addressee of a claim if the entitled person does not obtain the legitim from the heir (Article 1000 § 4 of the Civil Code). I have [discussed this whole mechanism in a separate text](/en/aktualnosci/family-foundation-and-legitim).

For planning purposes one conclusion matters. A fund contributed more than ten years before the opening of the succession is not added, unless the family foundation is an heir (Article 994¹ § 1 of the Civil Code). Time works in the heirs' favour here, but only where the decision is taken well in advance.

## Conclusions

A family foundation puts in order those components of assets that have been contributed to it. It replaces neither a will, nor well-considered articles of association, nor a succession manager for a sole proprietorship. When establishing one, it is therefore worth answering the following questions.

In what form is the operating activity carried out, and does the foundation actually hold shares in the company that carries it out.

What do that company's articles of association say about heirs joining, if any shares remain outside the foundation.

Who, under the statute, appoints the foundation's management board after the founder's death, and has a supervisory board been established.

Who will potentially be entitled to the legitim, and in what amount.

These matters are worth considering before taking any decision of a succession nature.
